StackBack, settings consolidation

Spec SET-01 v1 · one global layer for tokens and settings, with scoped overrides · built on the locked twelve-item nav (IA-01, D2026-0727) and the purchase-options settings grid · 2026-09-17

1. What the grid actually found

The purchase-options widget has 48 settings. They are stored in two different tables, edited on two different screens, and read by four different surfaces that do not agree on which store wins. That produces 81 editable controls for 48 decisions, and the extra 33 are not redundancy, they are a second opinion the merchant cannot see.

Three defect classes, all of them caused by the same thing

Dead controls, 8
default_intent, default_payment_mode, hide_onetime_option, hide_intent_selector, bundle_selector_tabs, promo_line, hide_free_shipping_line and direct_checkout are editable on the portal customize screen, save cleanly, and change nothing. The drawer never reads them.
A control that saves and does nothing is worse than a missing one. The merchant believes the change landed.
Silent divergence, 4
use_compare_at_price_for_discount_label, tab_discount_format and tag_shows_per_delivery_price exist in both stores and both are live, so the PDP and the drawer can show different discount maths for the same product. freebie_label_shows_value is read by the landing page but ignored by the drawer.
Nobody sees this in the admin. It only shows up as a customer complaint.
Locked open, 1
group_bundle_variants is OR-ed across both stores, so the widget editor cannot switch it off while the portal copy is on. The three hide_* payment flags have the same shape against a theme-editor checkbox.
An OR across two stores means one of the two screens is a decoration.
The cause is one decision, not thirteen bugs. The schema is already single-source: widget-templates.ts spreads the portal appearance schema rather than copying it, and says so in a comment. What was duplicated is the values, into shop_widgets.custom_settings and shop_portal_settings.custom_settings.widget, with each reader picking a side. Every defect above is downstream of that one split. Fixing the thirteen individually would leave the mechanism that produced them.

And three settings that never did anything at all

theme.chrome.borderVisible, theme.chrome.borderColor and theme.chrome.shadow compile to --sb-chrome-border and --sb-shadow, which have zero uses anywhere in the app. The landing template's own colors, typography and layout blocks have zero readers too: buildLandingCSSVars adds only alert variables on top of the widget theme and never consults them. Six schema blocks, no consumer.

2. The model: one resolver, four layers, one test

Stop asking "which store holds this setting". Ask "at what scope is this decision true", and let one resolver walk the layers. A value is never held in two places at the same level, and the layer a value came from is always visible.

LAYER 1
Product default
Shipped by us. What the widget does before a merchant touches anything.
widget_templates.default_settings
LAYER 2, NEW
Brand and Selling
The store's answer. Set once in Settings, read by every surface. This is where 36 of the 48 settings live.
shop_settings.brand / .selling
LAYER 3
Surface
Widget, Portal or Landing Pages as a class. Present in the resolver, deliberately empty at launch.
reserved
LAYER 4
Placement
This widget instance, this landing page. Holds its own copy, plus any override it has explicitly taken.
shop_widgets / shop_landing_pages
Nearest layer wins. Nothing is OR-ed, ever. The three OR-ed reads in the current build (the hide_* payment flags against the theme block, group_bundle_variants across both stores) become plain precedence. An OR is what makes a setting impossible to switch off from the screen that appears to own it.

The test that decides where a setting lives

One question, asked of every setting: would this store ever want a different answer on two placements?

NO Global, no override
The answer is a fact about the store, not a choice per placement. Whether your prices carry decimals. Whether your discounts are calculated off compare-at. Whether you are on a plan that lets you remove the branding.
12 settings. One control, in Settings. No override row is offered anywhere, because offering one is how the current mess started.
RARELY Global, opt-in override
Usually one answer, but a flash sale page in different colours or a bundle widget that opens on Bundle is legitimate. The global value is authoritative until a placement explicitly takes the override.
24 settings, in 8 groups. Overrides are taken by group, never by single key.
YES Placement only
Copy, and layout facts of the placement itself. A tab label. Whether the product row shows, which the drawer already answers differently because it draws its own header.
9 settings. No global control exists. The leaf is already the right scope.

Overrides are taken by group, not by key

A placement cannot override theme.borders.strong on its own. It overrides Surfaces, and all five keys move together. Per-key overriding is exactly what produced a PDP and a drawer that disagree on one flag with no way to see it. Group overrides keep a placement readable: it either matches the store or it visibly does not.

One resolver, and it returns its own provenance
resolve(shop, surface, placementId)
  => Record<key, { value, from }>

// from: "default" | "brand" | "selling"
// | "surface" | "placement"

Returning the layer alongside the value is what makes the admin honest. Every control renders an INHERITED or OVERRIDDEN marker from from rather than from a guess, and a storefront debug header can print the same thing. The current build cannot answer "why is this widget purple" without reading four files.

A setting renders only where it can take effect

Scope is already owned by the widget category in code: master, subscription, bundle, bundle-fixed, bundle-mix-and-match, onetime. group_bundle_variants is meaningless outside mix and match; bundle_selector_tabs outside bundles; compare_at_shipping_price on a free-shipping plan. These do not get a global control and they do not appear on placements that cannot use them. This is the second half of the simplification: the reason the current screens feel long is partly that they show every setting to every placement.

6. What it takes, in the order it has to happen

Six steps. The first two are worth doing whether or not the rest lands, because they fix live defects. Nothing here needs a nav change and nothing needs a migration that a pilot store can notice, as long as step 3 runs the way it is written.

  1. Take the free fixes first, before any refactor. Two blank-field bugs save 0 rather than the default: theme.shape.radius gives square corners and theme.typography.fontScale gives invisible text, both at app.widgets.$templateKey.tsx:188. Move the drawer sticky bar inside the themed wrapper and 12 theme keys start working. Wire the 7 alert colours that already have tokens. None of this depends on the settings model.
  2. Delete what has no reader. Three theme.chrome.* keys, the landing colors / typography / layout blocks. They are schema, defaults and admin controls for behaviour that does not exist. Deleting them shrinks the problem before it is modelled, and removes six blocks from the grid below.
  3. Run the resolver read-only, in parallel, for a week. Build resolve() over the four layers, seed the Brand and Selling layer from whichever store currently wins on each key, and have it log every disagreement with what production actually served. The answer to "which of Settings or Portal Settings is authoritative today", which IA-02 gated the Branding move on, is a diff, not a meeting. 22 pilot stores across 10 live ones is enough sample to settle it in days.
  4. Cut the write path over. Settings › Branding and Settings › Selling become the only screens that write those 36 keys. The portal widget tab stops writing and then stops rendering. shop_widgets.custom_settings keeps only the placement keys plus any override a placement has taken. The old blobs stay on disk unread until the diff log has been clean for a full billing cycle.
  5. Add the override card to the three leaf screens. Empty by default, one group at a time, always with a Reset to store. This is the only new UI in the whole spec, and it is one component used three times.
  6. Remove the theme-block checkboxes. data-hide-prepaid, data-hide-payg and data-hide-auto-debit in the Liquid are the last OR. They have to go after step 4, not before, or a merchant who set them in the theme editor loses the setting silently.

Open, and worth settling before build

  • Does Selling deserve its own rail item, or does it split across Orders, Payments and Policies? The argument for its own item is that all 19 keys are storefront presentation and none of them changes what is charged. The argument against is that a merchant looking for "hide pay as you go" will try Payments first. Search covers the second case, which is the reason IA-02 specced search alongside the rail.
  • Does default_payment_mode grow an auto_debit value. The stored enum is prepaid | pay_as_you_go, so AutoPay cannot be preselected on any store today even though it renders. Already flagged on 2026-09-17 in the Help Centre widget model. Product change, not a settings change.
  • Whether Brand and Selling are one rail item or two. Two is written here because 21 keys and 19 keys is a long single screen, and because Branding already exists in the IA by name. One item with two cards is defensible.
  • Where the surface layer gets used. It is in the resolver and empty at launch. The first real case is likely "all bundle widgets open on Bundle", which is a category rule rather than a per-instance one. Leaving the layer present and unused costs nothing; adding it later costs a migration.
The measure of success is not fewer settings. It is 48 settings with one home each, 12 of which a merchant sets once and can never diverge, and a leaf screen that shows what differs rather than what exists. The count of controls on the widget editor goes from 48 to 5. The count of decisions stays 48, because every one of them is a real decision somebody has to make.
